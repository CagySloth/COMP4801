## 12. Conclusion

This project investigated WhatsHap-based long-read phasing through a controlled synthetic benchmarking framework designed to balance realism, attribution, and experimental flexibility. The main objective was not only to measure phasing performance, but also to understand where performance is lost in practical end-to-end conditions and to identify optimization directions that are genuinely useful.

The study makes three main contributions.

First, it establishes a realistic and configurable benchmarking pipeline that supports both oracle and called regimes. This makes it possible to separate phaser-limited behaviour from full end-to-end behaviour and to attribute losses to variant recovery, phasing completeness, or phase correctness. The framework also supports multiple realism stressors, including duplicated regions, coverage dropout, correlated error bursts, variable read-length models, and truth indels with SNP-only safeguards. This experimental structure made it possible to move beyond simple performance reporting and instead study how different conditions affect WhatsHap-based phasing mechanisms.

Second, the experiments show that end-to-end performance is often limited less by the phasing algorithm itself than by the quality and overlap of informative heterozygous variants that survive upstream processing. In the oracle regime, WhatsHap remains highly accurate once correct sites are available, while the called regime often shows larger losses due to missing shared heterozygous sites and reduced connectivity. Among the isolated realism knobs, coverage dropout was the most impactful stressor, acting primarily through fragmentation and later also through reduced variant recovery. The duplication × dropout interaction further showed that realistic difficulty is often compounded rather than purely additive, with mixed caller-side and phaser-side weaknesses emerging most clearly under combined stress.

Third, the optimization study shows that useful but limited practical headroom exists. The strongest positive result came from caller-side base-quality tuning: reducing `call_min_baseq` to a moderate value improved call recall, shared heterozygous overlap, and end-to-end phased recall without materially harming precision. On the phasing side, the main lesson was to avoid overly strict filtering: `min_baseq = 20` was consistently harmful, whereas moderate values such as 10 lay on a stable performance plateau. Retained coverage also showed a practical runtime trade-off, with `max_coverage = 8` or `10` preserving performance while avoiding unnecessary compute. In contrast, caller and phasing mapping-quality thresholds provided little useful headroom and were effectively ruled out as major optimization levers.

The final recommended practical configuration from this study is therefore:
- `call_min_baseq = 10`
- `call_min_mapq = 20` (or default, since the effect was negligible)
- `max_coverage = 8`
- `min_mapq = 20`
- `min_baseq = 10`

A slightly more runtime-biased alternative with `max_coverage = 6` performed almost identically on the main phasing metrics and may be acceptable when efficiency is prioritized.

The project also provides an algorithm-facing result beyond parameter tuning alone. The SNP-density scaling study showed that the solve stage becomes progressively more expensive as the phasing problem grows, but also that the balanced tuned configuration scales substantially better than the default configuration. This suggests that practical tuning does not merely change final output metrics; it also changes the structure and difficulty of the residual wMEC-style problem presented to the solver.

At the same time, the project highlights an important methodological caution. Runtime profiling of the research pipeline showed that total phasing runtime is dominated by the custom readset-construction adaptor used for the experimental interface, rather than by the core solver itself. This adaptor was necessary to support oracle/called benchmarking, unified provenance, and controlled evaluation settings, but it is not the standard production-facing WhatsHap front-end. For this reason, the runtime results should be interpreted mainly as properties of the research pipeline and its controlled interface, not as a direct statement that the core WhatsHap solver is the dominant practical runtime bottleneck.

Overall, the central conclusion of this project is that WhatsHap is a strong and reliable phasing engine when informative variant evidence is available, but practical long-read phasing performance depends heavily on upstream variant recovery, connectivity, and the interaction of realistic stress conditions. Consequently, the most useful optimization directions are pipeline-level rather than purely solver-internal: preserve useful evidence, avoid overly conservative filtering, and improve the quality of the phasing problem that is ultimately passed to WhatsHap.

Future work can build on this study in several directions. One direction is to test the recommended configuration on broader and more realistic datasets, including real long-read data. Another is to investigate caller-side improvements more directly, since many end-to-end losses were shown to be overlap-limited rather than purely phaser-limited. A third is to examine the residual solver-facing problem more deeply, especially how connectivity, variant density, and evidence quality interact to affect the dynamic-programming stage. Finally, future work could compare the project-specific adaptor with the standard WhatsHap front-end directly, or optimize the adaptor separately if full pipeline runtime becomes a primary objective.

In summary, this project contributes a realistic benchmarking framework, a detailed attribution of WhatsHap-based phasing failures under controlled stress, and a practical set of experimentally supported optimization recommendations. These findings provide both a useful guide for practical WhatsHap use and a foundation for future work on more robust and efficient long-read phasing pipelines.
